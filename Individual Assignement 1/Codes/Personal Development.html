Personal Development

Financial freedom is important to me because I am a father to a young daughter, and providing a stable home for my family has long been one of my personal goals. When I was younger, I promised myself that when I had a child, that child would live under my roof and visit the grandparents, rather than my family continuing to live under my parents’ roof. This goal has influenced the way I assess major financial commitments. In particular, I compared the cost of renting with the cost of a mortgage and decided that, for my situation, taking on a mortgage was the better long-term choice.

Reading these chapters has encouraged me to think not only about how much money I earn, but also about what I do with it. My experience shows that I am willing to make long-term commitments, yet I still have to work on controlling the temptation to spend money that is readily available.

Seven Cures for a Lean Purse

In this chapter, Arkad teaches seven principles: save part of what you earn, control expenses, make savings produce income, protect money from loss, consider homeownership, provide for future needs, and develop the ability to earn more. Three cures stand out to me because they relate directly to decisions I have made and goals I am working toward.

The third cure, “Make thy gold multiply,” is particularly relevant to how I approach saving. I recognise that I am tempted to spend cash when I see something I want. Instead of leaving all of my available money where I can easily use it, I have chosen to invest in stocks. I began investing in the stock market in late 2019, and I describe the growth of my investment as moving from hundreds into millions. My experience has helped me appreciate the value of patience: I did not approach the stock market expecting to become wealthy overnight. At the same time, owning shares is not the same as keeping emergency cash, because share prices can rise or fall and money may be needed unexpectedly.

The fourth cure, “Guard thy treasures from loss,” connects with my caution about financial risk. I usually ask questions before committing myself to an investment or other arrangement because I want to understand what could go wrong. I have a family to support and do not believe it is wise to place my entire savings at risk without understanding the consequences. I also recognise that investment outcomes can change quickly, which makes it important to consider possible losses as well as gains.

The seventh cure, “Increase thy ability to earn,” also matters to me. Technology is changing workplaces and the skills employers require. This is one reason I am pursuing a Bachelor of Science in Computer Networking and Security. I have noticed how business families sometimes encourage their children to study subjects related to a family industry. To me, that shows how further education can introduce new knowledge and methods into an established business. In my own case, I hope to use my education to improve my professional opportunities and earning potential.

My decision to pursue a mortgage rather than rent illustrates the fifth cure, “Make of thy dwelling a profitable investment.” I wanted my daughter to grow up in a home I could call my own. Because I saw rental costs as being comparable with mortgage payments, ownership seemed more useful to my longer-term plans. The chapter helps me explain why I valued this decision: I was thinking about family stability and the future, not only about the next monthly payment. I recognise, however, that ownership also brings expenses and responsibilities beyond the mortgage itself.

Five Laws of Gold

The second chapter follows Nomasir, Arkad’s son, who is given gold and guidance but initially loses wealth through poor decisions. His later improvement highlights the difference between having money and knowing how to manage it. The five laws emphasise saving consistently, putting money to productive use, seeking experienced advice, avoiding unfamiliar investments, and rejecting promises of unrealistic returns.

For my circumstances, the third and fourth laws are especially important. The third law stresses the value of advice from knowledgeable people when protecting investments. The fourth warns against placing money into a business or arrangement one does not understand. These lessons connect with my habit of asking questions about risks before making a commitment.

I once considered a transaction involving an asset where I was unsure about the stability and legitimacy of the arrangement. During the process, I was encouraged to pay money to a person in government to have certain documents updated. That raised a serious concern, because I could have become involved in something unlawful. The transaction did not proceed: the asset was destroyed one day before the planned exchange of money. I cannot describe this outcome as proof that the arrangement would certainly have been illegal, but the circumstances were enough to show why I should not move forward when the process and risks are unclear.

Looking back, the lesson for me is that the desire to obtain an asset should never outweigh the need to understand how it is being transferred, whether its documents are legitimate, and whether the people involved are acting properly. That is the practical connection I see with the third and fourth laws. I would rather delay or abandon a transaction than expose myself, my finances or my family to avoidable legal and financial risks.

My Financial and Professional Goals

The financial habit I most want to improve is saving money in an account without immediately withdrawing it to buy something I want. At present, I often convert available cash into investments or other assets so that I have something of value instead of readily spendable money. Although investing is useful for long-term goals, I want to develop the discipline to maintain savings that remain available when genuinely needed.

Academically and professionally, completing my Bachelor of Science in Computer Networking and Security is an important part of my plan. I believe the qualification can broaden my opportunities in computing and technology, where new systems and methods continue to develop. I want to increase my knowledge rather than rely only on the skills I already have.

To protect my money, I intend to keep some savings in bank accounts that are not directly connected to payment cards. My reasoning is that reducing direct card access may reduce certain types of card-related exposure. I also need to remember that account security depends on more than card access, including strong authentication and safe banking practices.

My longer-term goal is to own multiple businesses in different industries. The idea appeals to me because I would not want to depend on only one business for income. Still, I would need to understand each industry and manage the risks carefully; having several businesses would not automatically guarantee financial stability.

My Biggest Takeaway

The most important lesson I take from the two chapters is that protecting money requires as much discipline as earning or investing it. I have seen growth in my stock investments, but I also recognise my tendency to spend cash when it is accessible and the danger of entering an arrangement before fully understanding it. Going forward, I want to separate money for everyday spending, accessible savings and longer-term investments, while asking more questions before making significant financial commitments.

This book helped me connect financial responsibility with choices I already face as a parent, student and investor. Homeownership has been part of my effort to provide stability for my family, investing has taught me patience, and continuing my education with University of Technology, Jamaica has shown my desire to improve my earning ability. The areas I still need to strengthen are accessible savings and caution in unfamiliar transactions. For me, the book’s main value is the reminder that long-term financial progress depends on repeated choices, not just on receiving more money.
